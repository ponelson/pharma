A medicine is a temporary monopoly that took fifteen years to build

Everything peculiar about the drug business follows from that. Nine in ten candidates fail, so the cost of a medicine is mostly the cost of the ones that did not work; the survivor is protected by patents and exclusivities that expire on a known date, after which most of its revenue vanishes in a year; and the price the world argues about is a list price that almost nobody pays. This guide starts at zero and follows the molecule from the lab through trials, approval, the factory, the wholesaler, the pharmacy and the PBM — and then follows the money, through a year in which the United States began setting drug prices by negotiation, by deal and by tariff.

01 / Orientation

What a drug company actually is

A business that spends a decade and a billion dollars converting a scientific hypothesis into a legally protected monopoly — and then watches the monopoly expire on a known date.

A medicine is two things at once. It is a molecule, made in a factory to a specification so exact that a deviation measured in parts per million can trigger a recall. And it is a bundle of legal rights — patents and regulatory exclusivities — that for a fixed number of years prevent anyone else from selling the same molecule. The molecule costs very little to make. The rights are what the company is selling.

That produces the industry's organising fact: the revenue curve is a cliff with a known date at the top. A successful drug launches, ramps over five to seven years to peak sales, holds there for a few more, and then — on the day exclusivity ends — loses most of its revenue within twelve to eighteen months as generic copies arrive at a small fraction of the price. Everything about how pharmaceutical companies are run, financed, valued and criticised follows from the need to refill that revenue before it falls off.

The second organising fact is that almost nothing about the price is set by the cost of making the drug. In the United States the published list price is a starting point for a chain of negotiated discounts, rebates and fees that can remove half or more of it before the manufacturer is paid. The patient's out-of-pocket cost, meanwhile, is often calculated on the list price nobody actually pays. Understanding the gap between list and net is the single most useful thing a newcomer to this industry can learn.

The two spines

Development: biology → target → molecule → preclinical → IND → Phase 1 → Phase 2 → Phase 3 → NDA / BLA → approval → launch → peak → loss of exclusivity → generic or biosimilar erosion
Distribution: key starting materials → API → finished dose → manufacturer → wholesaler → pharmacy, hospital or clinic → patient  ·  and in parallel the money: patient copay → plan → PBM → rebate back from manufacturer

The goods move down one chain and the money moves along a different one. A pill can travel from a plant in Ireland to a wholesaler in Ohio to a pharmacy in Brooklyn while the payment for it is split between a patient, an employer, an insurer, a pharmacy benefit manager, the federal government and the manufacturer's rebate department, settled months apart. Most of the industry's controversies — rebates, 340B, spread pricing, drug shortages — live in the mismatch between those two chains.

Four things that make pharma unlike other industries

1

Most of the investment fails

Roughly nine in ten candidates that enter human trials never reach patients. The cost of a successful drug is mostly the cost of the failures that preceded it — which is why "it only costs pennies to make" and "it cost $2 billion to develop" are both true.

2

The product is a time-limited legal right

Revenue is defined by patents and exclusivities with known expiry dates. No other large industry can predict, a decade ahead, the year in which its best product will lose most of its sales.

3

The buyer, the payer and the chooser are different people

The doctor chooses, the patient takes, the insurer pays, the PBM decides which drugs are covered, the employer funds it, and the government sets the rules for a third of it. Price competition in that structure works strangely, and sometimes backwards.

4

Governments set the price everywhere but America — and now there too

Most rich countries cap prices through health technology assessment and national negotiation. The US historically did not. Since 2022 Medicare negotiates; since 2025 the administration has used tariffs to extract international price parity. The American exception is closing.

02 / Discovery and modality

What a drug is made of

The industry used to mean small chemical pills. It now spans proteins, antibodies, engineered cells, genetic medicines and peptides — each with different economics.

Discovery starts with biology: a target — usually a protein — whose activity, if changed, should alter a disease. Chemists or biologists then search for something that binds the target and changes its behaviour, refine it through many rounds of design and testing, and select a development candidate with acceptable potency, safety and manufacturability. Most of the failure that kills drugs later is set in motion here: a target that does not actually drive the disease in humans will fail in Phase 2 no matter how elegant the molecule.

The modality — the kind of molecule — determines almost everything downstream: how it is made, how it is given, how long its exclusivity lasts, how fast copies erode it, and how the US government negotiates its price.

Chemistry, manufacturing and controls — the work of proving a drug can be made at scale, consistently, to specification — runs in parallel with clinical trials and is chronically underestimated by early-stage companies. A biotech with excellent Phase 2 data and no credible manufacturing process is worth much less than its press release suggests.
03 / Clinical development

Where the money goes, and mostly disappears

A decade of trials, each phase answering a different question, each with a failure rate that would bankrupt any other industry.

Multiply the column on the right and about one candidate in thirteen that enters Phase 1 becomes a medicine — fewer in oncology and neurology, more in haematology and rare diseases. Phase 2 is where most die, because it is the first real test of whether the biology was right. Phase 3 is where most of the money goes: a large cardiovascular or obesity outcomes trial can cost several hundred million dollars on its own.

Estimates of what it costs to bring one drug to market depend heavily on method. A 2020 study in JAMA using public filings put the median at just under $1 billion, including the cost of failures and capital; the Tufts Center's widely quoted 2016 figure, based on confidential company data and capitalised at a higher rate, was $2.6 billion. Both figures are mostly the cost of failure and of time. Neither says much about any particular drug.

Most trial operations are outsourced to contract research organisations — IQVIA, ICON, Thermo Fisher's PPD, Medpace, Fortrea — which recruit sites, monitor patients and manage data. Regulators have also built faster routes for important drugs: accelerated approval on a surrogate endpoint (tumour shrinkage rather than survival, say) with a confirmatory trial to follow; breakthrough therapy and fast track designations that bring more FDA interaction; and priority review, which cuts review time to six months and can be bought with a transferable voucher earned by developing drugs for rare paediatric or tropical diseases.

The model also shows why big pharma prefers to buy later-stage assets at much higher prices: it is paying to skip the probabilities it cannot diversify away.

04 / Regulation and approval

The regulator as gatekeeper

Approval is not a scientific verdict alone. It is a legal permission to market a specific product, for specific uses, made in specific plants.

In the US, the Food and Drug Administration's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) reviews most drugs; its Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) handles vaccines, blood products and cell and gene therapies. A company files a New Drug Application (NDA) for a small molecule or a Biologics License Application (BLA) for a biologic. User fees paid by companies under the Prescription Drug User Fee Act (PDUFA) fund much of the review staff and commit the FDA to target dates — the PDUFA date that investors circle on calendars.

The FDA can approve, or issue a complete response letter listing deficiencies — clinical, statistical or manufacturing. Some applications go to an advisory committee of outside experts whose vote is not binding but is closely watched. Approval comes with a label defining the indications, dosing and warnings the company may promote; promoting off-label uses is illegal, though doctors may prescribe off-label freely. Many approvals carry post-marketing requirements, and some carry a REMS — a risk management programme that can restrict who may prescribe or dispense the drug.

Outside the US, the European Medicines Agency runs a centralised procedure for most innovative drugs, after which pricing and reimbursement are negotiated country by country — approval and access are separate steps in Europe in a way they are not in the US. Japan's PMDA and China's NMPA are the other major regulators; China's reforms since 2015 turned it from a laggard into a major source of new molecules.

Manufacturing is regulated as tightly as the molecule. Every plant that makes an approved product must follow current Good Manufacturing Practice and is subject to inspection. A Form 483 lists an inspector's observations; a warning letter escalates them; an import alert can bar a foreign plant's products from the US entirely. Many generic shortages begin with a quality failure at a single plant in India or China that supplied a large share of the US market.

05 / Patents and exclusivity

The monopoly and its expiry date

A drug's protection comes from two separate systems — patents from the patent office and exclusivities from the regulator — and the later of them to expire sets the cliff.

A patent runs twenty years from filing. Because drugs are patented early and take a decade to develop, the effective patent life left at launch is often only ten to twelve years. US law restores some of it: patent term extension can add up to five years for regulatory delay, capped at fourteen years of protection after approval. Companies also file later patents on formulations, dosing regimens, manufacturing methods and new uses, which can extend protection for a modified product or create litigation risk for generic entrants.

Separately, the FDA grants regulatory exclusivities that block or delay copies regardless of patents. They are the backbone of protection for many drugs, and the protection biotechs model when they build a revenue forecast.

Hatch-Waxman and the generic bargain

The 1984 Hatch-Waxman Act created the modern generic industry. It let generics win approval by showing bioequivalence rather than repeating trials, and in exchange gave brand companies patent term restoration and the exclusivities above. Brands list relevant patents in the FDA's Orange Book. A generic company can file a Paragraph IV certification asserting a listed patent is invalid or not infringed; if the brand sues within 45 days, approval is automatically stayed for up to 30 months while the courts decide. The first generic to file a successful challenge earns 180 days of exclusivity against other generics — the prize that makes patent challenges worth funding.

The 2010 Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act created an equivalent pathway for biosimilars, with the Purple Book as its reference list and a structured, and famously complicated, exchange of patent information known as the patent dance.

Where the system gets gamed. Brands have used patent thickets — AbbVie built well over a hundred patents around Humira, delaying US biosimilars until 2023 despite European competition from 2018. Product hopping moves patients to a reformulated version just before generics arrive. Pay-for-delay settlements, in which the brand pays a generic challenger to stay off the market, have been subject to antitrust scrutiny since the Supreme Court's 2013 Actavis decision. Citizen petitions to the FDA can delay generic approvals. Each is legal in some form; each is the subject of regular legislative and FTC attention.
06 / Manufacturing and supply chain

Where medicines are actually made

The world's medicine cabinet is assembled in a handful of countries, for reasons of tax as much as cost — and the cheapest drugs have the most fragile supply chains.

A pill begins as key starting materials, chemical building blocks that are processed through a sequence of reactions into the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API). The API is then formulated with inactive ingredients into a finished dose — a tablet, capsule, injection — and packaged. For biologics, the API is grown in cell cultures in stainless-steel or single-use bioreactors, purified, and then filled into vials or syringes under sterile conditions in a separate "fill-finish" step. Each step can happen in a different country.

The geography divides cleanly by product type. Branded drugs and biologics are concentrated in the US, Ireland, Switzerland, Germany, Belgium, Denmark, Singapore and Puerto Rico — locations chosen as much for tax treatment of the intellectual property as for labour or skills. Generics are made predominantly in India, which supplies a large share — by most estimates around 40% — of US generic prescriptions by volume, using APIs and starting materials that very often come from China. A significant share of the world's supply of some antibiotics and older generic APIs comes from a handful of Chinese plants.

Outsourcing

CDMOs

Contract development and manufacturing organisations make drugs for companies that lack the plants or the scale: Lonza, Samsung Biologics, Thermo Fisher, Fujifilm Diosynth, WuXi Biologics, and Catalent (bought by Novo Holdings in 2024 largely for GLP-1 fill-finish capacity). Most biotechs own no factories at all.

Policy

BIOSECURE and China

US legislation to restrict federal contracting with designated Chinese biotechnology suppliers advanced through 2024 and was enacted in revised form through defence legislation at the end of 2025, by most reports with a multi-year phase-in. Companies have been dual-sourcing away from Chinese CDMOs ever since.

Policy

Tariffs and onshoring

The 2026 Section 232 tariff on patented drugs exempts companies that commit to US manufacturing and MFN pricing. Large manufacturers have announced US investment programmes in the tens of billions of dollars each since 2025. Plants take three to five years to build and validate, so the supply effect lags the announcements by years.

Fragility

Drug shortages

US shortages, which ASHP counted above 300 active in early 2024, are concentrated in old, cheap, sterile injectables — chemotherapy agents, anaesthetics, saline. The cause is economic: when a generic sells for cents and margins are near zero, no one invests in redundant capacity or quality, and one plant's failure becomes a national shortage.

Track and trace. The US Drug Supply Chain Security Act of 2013 requires that every prescription package carry a serial number and that each sale be recorded electronically, so that a package can be traced from manufacturer to pharmacy and counterfeits detected. Its final, interoperable requirements came in under phased FDA exemptions — for manufacturers and wholesalers in 2025, for larger dispensers in late 2025 and for small dispensers in November 2026. Biologics add a cold chain: most must be kept between 2°C and 8°C from plant to patient, and some cell therapies are shipped in liquid nitrogen.

07 / Distribution

How a pill gets to a pharmacy

Three wholesalers move nearly all of America's medicines on margins thinner than a grocery store's — and earn their money in ways that are not obvious from the margin.

US drug distribution is one of the most concentrated industries in the economy. McKesson, Cencora (formerly AmerisourceBergen) and Cardinal Health together handle the overwhelming majority of prescription drugs sold in the US, each with revenues of a few hundred billion dollars a year and operating margins of one to two percent. They buy from manufacturers, warehouse in regional distribution centres, and deliver daily to pharmacies, hospitals and clinics.

The thin margin hides a better business than it appears. Wholesalers are paid fee-for-service by brand manufacturers — a percentage of list price under distribution service agreements, in exchange for data and inventory discipline. They earn far more on generics, where they buy at the bottom of a deflationary market through giant sourcing ventures (Red Oak, ClarusOne and others) and resell at a spread. They run specialty distribution for oncology and rheumatology practices, own physician-practice networks, and settled the national opioid litigation in 2021–22 for about $21 billion.

The 340B programme, created in 1992, requires manufacturers selling to Medicaid to give steep discounts to qualifying safety-net providers. Those providers are reimbursed by insurers at normal rates, and keep the difference to fund care for poorer patients. As it grew — particularly through thousands of contract pharmacies — manufacturers began restricting contract-pharmacy discounts and pushing for a rebate model in which they pay discounts after verifying eligibility. Federal regulators started a limited rebate pilot for 2026. Hospitals regard the programme as a lifeline; manufacturers regard much of it as arbitrage.
08 / Pricing

List price is a fiction everyone agrees to use

The number in the headline, the number the pharmacy is paid and the number the manufacturer keeps are three different numbers — and the patient is often charged on the largest.

The US price vocabulary is a set of benchmarks, each used by a different party. WAC, wholesale acquisition cost, is the manufacturer's published list price to wholesalers. AWP, average wholesale price, is a published figure roughly 20% above WAC that many pharmacy contracts still reference. AMP, average manufacturer price, drives Medicaid rebates. ASP, average sales price net of most discounts, sets Medicare Part B payment. And the net price — what the manufacturer actually keeps after every rebate, fee and discount — is confidential, reported only in aggregate.

The difference between list and net is the gross-to-net gap. For brand-name drugs in aggregate it exceeds $300 billion a year on Drug Channels Institute estimates, and for heavily rebated categories — insulin, inhalers, anticoagulants — net prices can be a third of list or less. The gap grew for two decades because the system rewarded it: PBMs were paid partly as a percentage of rebates, rebates were percentages of list price, and formulary access went to whoever offered the largest rebate. A higher list price with a bigger rebate could win the formulary over a lower list price with a smaller one.

A commercially insured patient in a deductible or coinsurance phase pays a share of the list price, while the plan's real cost is the net price after rebates — so the patient can end up paying a larger share of the drug's true cost than the plan does. That arithmetic is the core of the political case against rebates, and the reason recent reforms push cost-sharing toward net price.

Manufacturers fill part of the gap with copay assistance — coupons that cover a patient's out-of-pocket cost for a branded drug. Insurers and PBMs responded with accumulator and maximiser programmes that stop the coupon counting toward the patient's deductible, so the patient's costs resume once the coupon runs out. Copay assistance is banned in federal programmes as an illegal inducement.

09 / Payers and PBMs

The middlemen, and why they are the middle

Pharmacy benefit managers sit between every plan, every pharmacy and every manufacturer. Three of them handle roughly four-fifths of US prescriptions, and each is owned by a health insurer.

A PBM administers the prescription-drug benefit for an employer, a health plan or a Medicare Part D sponsor. It builds the formulary — the list of covered drugs and their cost-sharing tiers; negotiates rebates from manufacturers in exchange for formulary position; contracts a pharmacy network and sets what each pharmacy is reimbursed; processes claims in real time at the pharmacy counter; and runs its own mail-order and specialty pharmacies. The three largest — CVS Caremark (CVS Health, which also owns Aetna), Express Scripts (Cigna) and Optum Rx (UnitedHealth Group) — manage about 80% of US prescription claims.

Critics point to three practices. Spread pricing: charging a plan more for a generic than the PBM pays the pharmacy and keeping the difference. Rebate retention and fees tied to list price, which reward high list prices. And vertical integration: the same parent company owns the insurer, the PBM, the specialty pharmacy, and in UnitedHealth's case a large network of physicians — so it can steer patients to its own businesses and book profit wherever it is least visible. Starting in 2019 each of the big three also set up offshore group purchasing organisations to negotiate rebates, which made the flows harder still to follow.

Reform, finally

Law

Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026

Signed in February 2026, it delinks PBM compensation from drug prices in Medicare Part D — flat, service-based fees in place of percentages of list price — and requires much more detailed reporting to employer plan sponsors.

Enforcement

FTC settlements

The FTC sued the big three in 2024 over insulin rebating. Express Scripts settled in February 2026 and CVS Caremark in July, agreeing to delink their own compensation from list prices, base patient cost-sharing on net prices, stop favouring high-list-price versions of the same drug, and bring their offshore GPOs back to the US. Optum Rx reached a proposed settlement expected to follow the same template. None paid a fine or admitted wrongdoing.

Market

Business model shifts

Express Scripts announced a move away from rebate-based pricing, Optum Rx a fee-based model with full rebate pass-through, and in August 2026 ten PBMs agreed to show their negotiated plan prices alongside cash prices on TrumpRx. Critics expect lost rebate income to reappear as administrative fees.

States

Ownership limits

Arkansas passed a law in 2025 barring PBMs from owning pharmacies in the state; it was immediately challenged in court. A bipartisan Senate bill would go further and separate PBMs from insurers and providers entirely.

Medicare Part D was redesigned by the Inflation Reduction Act. From 2025 beneficiaries' annual out-of-pocket costs are capped — at $2,000, indexed to $2,100 in 2026 — and manufacturers fund a discount of 10% in the initial coverage phase and 20% in the catastrophic phase. Plans now carry much more of the cost of expensive drugs, which makes them more aggressive formulary managers and changes which drugs are worth rebating.
10 / Government pricing

The end of the American exception

Every other rich country sets drug prices through national bodies. Since 2022 the US has built three separate machines to do something similar.

1. Medicare negotiation

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 let Medicare negotiate prices for a growing list of high-spend drugs that have been on the market for years without competition: small molecules become eligible nine years after approval, biologics thirteen. The first ten negotiated prices — for Eliquis, Jardiance, Xarelto, Januvia, Farxiga, Entresto, Enbrel, Imbruvica, Stelara and NovoLog — took effect on 1 January 2026. Fifteen more, including semaglutide at $274 a month, take effect on 1 January 2027; CMS estimated those prices would have cut net spending on the fifteen by about 36%. The third round, the first to include physician-administered Part B drugs, is due to be published by the end of 2026 for 2028. Industry argues the shorter clock for pills — the "pill penalty" — distorts R&D toward biologics; Congress has already widened the orphan-drug exclusion.

2. Most-favoured-nation deals and TrumpRx

From September 2025 the administration negotiated company-by-company agreements under which manufacturers offer Medicaid prices matched to the lowest among peer rich countries, launch new drugs at prices aligned internationally, and sell directly to cash-paying consumers at discounted prices through TrumpRx.gov, which launched in February 2026. Pfizer signed first; by 31 August 2026, 26 manufacturers covering roughly 89% of the branded market had signed. The terms are largely confidential, which has made their real effect on net prices hard to measure. The GLP-1 deals with Lilly and Novo Nordisk set Medicare and Medicaid prices for obesity drugs at $245 a month and opened Medicare coverage for weight loss through a temporary bridge programme with a $50 copay from July 2026.

3. Tariffs as a pricing tool

On 2 April 2026, after a Section 232 national-security investigation, the President imposed a 100% tariff on imported patented drugs and their ingredients. Companies with a Commerce-approved US onshoring plan pay 20%, rising to 100% in 2030; companies with both an onshoring plan and an MFN pricing deal pay nothing through 20 January 2029. Generics and biosimilars are exempt, as are UK-made products under the US–UK arrangement, and some trading partners' rates are capped by bilateral deals. The tariff took effect on 31 July for the 17 largest companies — all of which had signed deals and so pay nothing — and on 29 September for everyone else, where it falls hardest on smaller biotechs importing product made by overseas contract manufacturers. Separately, USTR has opened Section 301 investigations into foreign drug-pricing practices, including Germany's.

11 / Economics and valuation

A business built on refilling a leaking bucket

Gross margins of 70–85%, R&D of 15–25% of sales, and a revenue base that falls off a scheduled cliff every few years.

A large pharmaceutical company's income statement is distinctive. Cost of goods is small — often 15–30% of sales — because the molecule is cheap and the price is set by its exclusivity. R&D is large, typically a fifth of revenue. Sales and marketing is large too, though the field-sales army of the 2000s has shrunk. The resulting operating margins, often 30–40% before amortisation of acquired intangibles, look extraordinary until you remember that the revenue they rest on is temporary.

The defining strategic problem is the patent cliff. Between now and the end of the decade, products with well over $200 billion of annual sales lose US exclusivity — among them Keytruda, Eliquis and Opdivo around 2028. A company facing a cliff has three choices: develop its own replacements, buy them, or shrink. In practice it does all three, which is why pharmaceutical M&A runs in waves timed to expiry calendars, and why large companies pay such high prices for late-stage assets in crowded areas.

The patent cliff: what happens when copies arrive

A blockbuster loses exclusivity. Generic or biosimilar entrants price below the brand, payers switch, and the market's value collapses — mostly to the benefit of payers. The number of entrants does most of the work.

How deals are structured

Licensing

Upfront, milestones, royalties

A biotech licenses a molecule to a large company for an upfront payment, milestone payments triggered by clinical and regulatory events, and royalties on sales. The headline "deal value" adds all milestones — "biobucks" — most of which are never paid.

M&A

Bolt-ons and transformations

Most acquisitions are bolt-ons of a few billion dollars for one or two late-stage assets; transformational mergers (Pfizer–Wyeth, BMS–Celgene, AbbVie–Allergan) are rarer and usually defensive responses to a cliff.

Royalty finance

Selling the future stream

Royalty Pharma and others buy existing royalties or fund trials in exchange for a share of future sales. A sophisticated, non-dilutive form of capital whose price tells you what professionals think a drug is worth.

China

The new supply of molecules

Chinese biotechs went from copying to originating in a decade. By 2025, by several counts, they accounted for around a third of the molecules large Western companies in-licensed, often at far lower upfront prices than US equivalents.

Venture

Biotech funding cycle

Early biotechs are funded by venture capital, then IPOs and follow-on equity, then partnerships. Funding is highly cyclical; when the IPO window shuts, companies cut programmes or sell.

Valuation

Sum of the parts

A pharma company is valued as the rNPV of its marketed products plus its pipeline, less debt. A biotech is often valued as cash plus the option value of one or two assets; trading below cash is common and not necessarily cheap.

12 / Tax and structure

Where pharmaceutical profits are booked

Intellectual property is the most mobile asset in the economy. The industry has used that mobility to keep its effective tax rates far below its home countries' headline rates.

The value of a drug lies in its patents and know-how, and those can be owned by a subsidiary anywhere. For decades the standard structure placed IP ownership — and therefore most of the profit — in low-tax jurisdictions such as Ireland, Switzerland, Singapore and, until a 1996 repeal phased out over ten years, Puerto Rico. The US parent and its foreign IP owner shared development costs under cost-sharing agreements, transfer-pricing rules governed what each paid the other, and US sales were often made from product manufactured offshore and sold to the US distributor at a high transfer price. The 2014–16 "inversion" wave, in which US companies redomiciled abroad through mergers, ended with Treasury rules that killed the Pfizer–Allergan deal.

Successive reforms narrowed the gap. The 2017 tax act cut the US rate to 21% and introduced minimum taxes on foreign intangible income (GILTI) and a lower rate on export income from US-held IP (FDII). The OECD's Pillar Two global minimum tax of 15% took effect in much of the world from 2024 — Ireland's rate for large groups rose from 12.5% to 15% — though the G7 agreed in 2025 to a "side-by-side" arrangement for US-parented groups. The 2025 US budget law renamed and adjusted the foreign-income regimes and restored immediate expensing of domestic R&D. Tariffs then added a second reason to manufacture in the US: the onshoring announcements of 2025–26 respond to the tax code and the tariff together.

1

R&D tax credit

The US research credit, plus immediate expensing of domestic research costs restored in 2025, lowers the after-tax cost of trials run in the US.

2

Orphan drug credit

A credit for clinical testing costs of drugs for rare diseases — cut from 50% to 25% in 2017. Combined with seven years of orphan exclusivity and higher prices, it made rare diseases one of the industry's most profitable niches.

3

Transfer pricing

The price a company charges itself for product and IP across borders decides where profit lands. It is the most litigated area of pharma tax, with multibillion-dollar disputes running for a decade.

4

Tariff interaction

A tariff is levied on customs value — often the internal transfer price. Companies with high transfer prices into the US face proportionally higher tariff bills, which is now a reason to revisit structures built for income tax.

13 / Tricks of the trade

The promotional playbook

Pharma promotion rarely fabricates data. It selects, frames and extrapolates — and the most important information is usually in what a press release leaves out.

Data

"Met its primary endpoint"

Statistically significant is not the same as clinically meaningful. Ask for the effect size, the confidence interval, the comparator and the absolute rather than relative benefit.

Data

Subgroup salvage

When a trial fails overall, a press release finds a subgroup in which it "worked". Unless the subgroup was pre-specified and powered, it is a hypothesis, not a result.

Data

Surrogate endpoints

Tumour shrinkage, biomarker changes and progression-free survival can be approved on, and later fail to translate into longer or better lives. Several accelerated approvals have been withdrawn after confirmatory trials failed.

Data

Single-arm and cross-trial comparisons

No control group, or a comparison with a different trial in different patients. Common in rare disease and oncology, and frequently flattering.

Data

Topline before the data

A positive "topline" announcement can precede full results by months. The full data, presented at a medical meeting, sometimes tell a less exciting story.

Deals

Biobucks

A "$2 billion deal" may be $50 million upfront and $1.95 billion of milestones that are mostly never paid. The upfront payment is the market price.

Forecasts

Peak sales and TAM

Total addressable market multiplied by an optimistic share is not a forecast. Compare against what recent launches in similar indications actually achieved.

Forecasts

Ignoring the exclusivity date

A forecast that runs peak sales flat beyond loss of exclusivity — or ignores a competitor's likely launch — overstates value. Find the LOE date and check the model honours it.

Pricing

List-price cuts that change nothing

A widely publicised list-price cut may coincide with an equivalent cut in rebates, leaving net price unchanged. It can still lower costs for patients paying on list, which is often the real target.

Channel

Channel stuffing

Shipping extra inventory to wholesalers ahead of a price rise inflates a quarter's sales. Distribution agreements limit it; watch for wholesaler inventory commentary and unusual revenue spikes before increases.

Access

Copay programmes as price protection

Coupons keep patients on a brand when a cheaper equivalent exists, shifting cost to plans. Useful for patients; also a deliberate defence against generic substitution.

Market

Compounded and grey-market copies

When branded GLP-1s were in shortage, compounding pharmacies sold copies legally; once shortages ended, many continued. Products sold outside FDA-approved channels carry quality risks and are a recurring enforcement target.

Two questions that do most of the work

What is the absolute benefit, against what comparator, on what endpoint? Most overstated claims in pharma dissolve when the relative risk reduction is converted into an absolute one and the surrogate endpoint is named.

When does exclusivity end, and what is the net price? Revenue forecasts that are honest about the cliff date and the gross-to-net discount are rare enough to be a signal of a trustworthy model.

15 / The present moment

Where the industry stands in October 2026

Tariffs, negotiated prices, PBM reform and the GLP-1 economy, all landing at once.

1. Trade policy became drug-pricing policy

The Section 232 tariff on patented drugs is now fully in force, but for the largest companies it is a lever rather than a cost: all seventeen companies on the first implementation list signed MFN and onshoring deals and pay nothing through January 2029. The burden falls on smaller biotechs that import product made by overseas CDMOs and have neither the leverage nor the US plants to negotiate exemptions; their trade group has complained of unclear exemption procedures. The February 2026 Supreme Court ruling that IEEPA cannot be used to impose tariffs did not touch Section 232, which is precisely why the administration chose it.

2. Three price-setting systems are running in parallel

Medicare's IRA prices for ten drugs took effect in January; fifteen more, including semaglutide, arrive in January 2027; and the third list, the first with Part B drugs, is due for publication by the end of this year. MFN deals now cover most of the branded market, with prices that are mostly confidential. And TrumpRx lists cash prices that are sometimes below Medicare's negotiated price and sometimes above it — Eliquis's negotiated Medicare price is below its TrumpRx cash price, semaglutide's is above Medicare's MFN price. No one yet has a clean view of what net US prices will be in 2028.

3. PBMs are being rewired

The Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 delinked PBM pay from drug prices in Part D. The FTC settled with Express Scripts in February and CVS Caremark in July, with Optum Rx's proposed settlement expected to follow; changes must be in place in standard offerings by January 2027. All three have moved toward fee-based, pass-through models, and in August ten PBMs agreed to list their negotiated prices on TrumpRx. Vertical integration — insurer, PBM and pharmacy under one roof — remains untouched at the federal level.

4. The GLP-1 economy

Obesity drugs are reshaping the industry's finances and its policy. Medicare began covering weight-loss GLP-1s for the first time in July 2026 through a bridge programme at a $245 net price and a $50 copay, running to the end of 2027. Novo Nordisk will cut semaglutide's US list price to $675 a month on 1 January 2027, the day Medicare's negotiated price takes effect. Oral versions are arriving, cash-pay direct-to-consumer channels have grown quickly, and compounded copies remain a regulatory battleground.

5. The cliff

Behind the policy noise sits the calendar. Keytruda, Eliquis, Opdivo and a string of other blockbusters lose US exclusivity around 2028. The scale of the revenue at risk explains the pace of 2025–26 dealmaking, the appetite for Chinese-originated molecules, and the willingness of large companies to sign pricing deals that protect their remaining exclusive years from tariffs.

1

IRA round three

The first negotiated Part B prices, due by year-end, will show how far the programme reaches into oncology and immunology.

2

Biotech tariff exposure

Whether smaller companies get workable exemption routes, or move manufacturing, before tariff costs reach patients.

3

PBM implementation

Whether delinking and FTC terms change net costs for plans, or reappear as fees.

4

MFN transparency

Any disclosure of the actual terms would let analysts measure what the deals did to net prices.

Read every price with its channel attached. In 2026 the same GLP-1 can have a list price, a commercial net price, a Medicaid MFN price, a Medicare bridge price, a TrumpRx cash price and a 2027 negotiated price — six numbers for one molecule. A claim about "the price" of a drug that does not name the channel is not yet a claim.
16 / Vocabulary

The words, defined

Filter by category or search. Pharmaceutical vocabulary is dense, legalistic and frequently acronymised; the definitions note where a term means something different to different parties.

TargetDevelopment

A biological molecule, usually a protein, whose activity a drug is designed to change.

ModalityDevelopment

The type of molecule: small molecule, antibody, peptide, cell therapy, gene therapy, RNA, vaccine.

Small moleculeDevelopment

A chemically synthesised drug, usually a pill. Easy to copy exactly once exclusivity ends.

BiologicDevelopment

A drug made in living cells — antibodies, proteins, vaccines. Hard to copy exactly; copies are biosimilars.

ADCDevelopment

Antibody–drug conjugate: an antibody that delivers a toxic payload to cells carrying a specific marker.

CAR-TDevelopment

A cell therapy in which a patient's T cells are engineered to attack cancer, then reinfused.

GLP-1 agonistDevelopment

A class of peptide drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide) for diabetes and obesity. The largest commercial category in the industry.

Development candidateDevelopment

The molecule selected to move into formal preclinical development.

CMCDevelopment

Chemistry, manufacturing and controls: proving a drug can be made consistently at scale.

INDDevelopment

Investigational New Drug application: FDA permission to start human trials.

Phase 1 / 2 / 3Development

Successive stages of human testing: safety and dose, then efficacy signal, then confirmatory efficacy at scale.

EndpointDevelopment

The outcome a trial measures. A primary endpoint determines whether the trial succeeded.

Surrogate endpointDevelopment

A measure, such as a biomarker or tumour shrinkage, used in place of a clinical outcome like survival.

Placebo-controlledDevelopment

A trial comparing the drug with an inactive treatment. The cleanest test, not always ethical.

Probability of success (PoS)Development

The chance a drug advances from one phase to the next, or to approval. About 8% from Phase 1 overall.

CRODevelopment

Contract research organisation that runs trials for sponsors.

BiomarkerDevelopment

A measurable indicator of disease or response, used to select patients or as a surrogate endpoint.

NDA / BLARegulation

New Drug Application and Biologics License Application — the filings for approval of a new drug or biologic.

ANDARegulation

Abbreviated New Drug Application for a generic, based on bioequivalence.

505(b)(2)Regulation

A hybrid approval route for modified versions of existing drugs, relying partly on existing data.

BiosimilarRegulation

A biologic shown to have no clinically meaningful difference from an approved reference product.

InterchangeableRegulation

A biosimilar designation allowing pharmacists to substitute it without the prescriber's involvement.

PDUFA dateRegulation

The FDA's target date to act on an application under the user-fee programme.

Complete response letterRegulation

FDA's notice that an application cannot be approved as submitted, listing the deficiencies.

Advisory committeeRegulation

A panel of outside experts that advises FDA on an application. Its vote is not binding.

Accelerated approvalRegulation

Approval based on a surrogate endpoint, conditional on a confirmatory trial.

Breakthrough therapyRegulation

FDA designation for drugs showing substantial early improvement, bringing intensive guidance.

Priority review voucherRegulation

A transferable voucher, earned for certain rare paediatric or tropical disease approvals, that buys a six-month review. Sells for around $100 million.

LabelRegulation

The approved prescribing information: indications, dosing, warnings. Defines what a company may promote.

REMSRegulation

Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy — FDA-required controls on prescribing or dispensing a risky drug.

cGMPRegulation

Current Good Manufacturing Practice — the regulations governing drug manufacturing quality.

Form 483 / warning letterRegulation

An inspector's list of observations, and FDA's formal escalation when they are not resolved.

Import alertRegulation

FDA action allowing detention of a foreign facility's products at the border.

HTARegulation

Health technology assessment: evaluation of a drug's value for money, used to set or approve prices outside the US.

QALYRegulation

Quality-adjusted life year, the unit of benefit used in many HTA cost-effectiveness thresholds.

Loss of exclusivity (LOE)IP & exclusivity

The date after which copies can enter. The cliff at the end of every drug's revenue curve.

Patent term extensionIP & exclusivity

Up to five years of additional US patent life to compensate for regulatory review time.

NCE exclusivityIP & exclusivity

Five years during which no generic of a new chemical entity may be filed.

Orphan exclusivityIP & exclusivity

Seven years of US market exclusivity for a drug treating a disease affecting fewer than 200,000 Americans.

Paediatric exclusivityIP & exclusivity

Six months added to existing protection in return for completing FDA-requested paediatric studies.

Orange BookIP & exclusivity

FDA's list of approved small-molecule drugs and the patents that protect them.

Purple BookIP & exclusivity

FDA's database of licensed biologics and their exclusivity information.

Paragraph IVIP & exclusivity

A generic applicant's certification that a listed patent is invalid or not infringed, triggering litigation.

30-month stayIP & exclusivity

The automatic delay in generic approval when a brand sues after a Paragraph IV filing.

180-day exclusivityIP & exclusivity

The reward to the first generic filer that successfully challenges a patent.

Patent thicketIP & exclusivity

A dense web of secondary patents around one product, designed to make challenges slow and expensive.

EvergreeningIP & exclusivity

Extending effective protection through new patents on minor modifications.

Product hoppingIP & exclusivity

Switching patients to a new protected version of a drug before generics of the old one arrive.

Pay-for-delayIP & exclusivity

A settlement in which a brand pays a generic challenger to delay entry. Subject to antitrust review since 2013.

Authorised genericIP & exclusivity

A generic version sold under the brand's own approval, usually through a partner, to capture part of the generic market.

APIManufacturing

Active pharmaceutical ingredient — the substance in a drug that produces its effect.

Key starting materialManufacturing

The chemical building blocks from which an API is synthesised. Often made in China.

Fill-finishManufacturing

The sterile filling of a drug into vials or syringes. A frequent capacity bottleneck.

CDMOManufacturing

Contract development and manufacturing organisation that makes drugs for other companies.

Cold chainManufacturing

Temperature-controlled storage and transport, usually 2–8°C, required for most biologics.

DSCSAManufacturing

Drug Supply Chain Security Act: US serialisation and electronic tracing for prescription drugs.

Drug shortageManufacturing

Supply below demand for a medicine, concentrated in low-priced sterile generics.

WholesalerDistribution

A distributor that buys from manufacturers and delivers to pharmacies and providers. Three dominate the US.

Specialty pharmacyDistribution

A pharmacy dispensing high-cost, complex drugs with handling and patient support.

GPODistribution

Group purchasing organisation negotiating supply contracts for hospitals.

Buy and billDistribution

Providers buy physician-administered drugs, then bill the insurer after administration.

340BDistribution

A federal programme requiring manufacturer discounts to qualifying safety-net providers.

Contract pharmacyDistribution

A retail pharmacy dispensing 340B drugs on behalf of a covered entity. The most contested part of 340B.

Fee-for-service (distribution)Distribution

Payments from manufacturers to wholesalers, usually a percentage of list price, for distribution services.

WACPricing

Wholesale acquisition cost: the manufacturer's list price to wholesalers.

AWPPricing

Average wholesale price: a published benchmark, roughly 20% above WAC, still used in pharmacy contracts.

AMPPricing

Average manufacturer price paid by retail pharmacies; the basis of Medicaid rebates.

ASPPricing

Average sales price net of most discounts; Medicare Part B pays ASP plus 6%.

Net pricePricing

What the manufacturer keeps after all rebates, discounts and fees.

Gross-to-netPricing

The difference between list and net price. Exceeds $300 billion a year across US brands.

RebatePricing

A payment from manufacturer to PBM or plan after a prescription is filled, usually in exchange for formulary position.

ChargebackPricing

A payment from manufacturer to wholesaler covering the difference between WAC and a lower contract price to a provider.

Best pricePricing

The lowest price a manufacturer gives most US commercial buyers; it sets a floor for Medicaid rebates.

Medicaid rebatePricing

Statutory rebate of 23.1% of AMP for brands plus an inflation penalty; uncapped since 2024.

Copay assistancePricing

Manufacturer coupons covering a patient's out-of-pocket cost. Banned in federal programmes.

Accumulator / maximiserPricing

Plan designs that stop copay assistance counting toward a patient's deductible or spread it across the year.

MFN pricingPricing

Most-favoured-nation pricing: matching US prices to the lowest among peer rich countries.

PBMPayers

Pharmacy benefit manager: administers drug benefits, builds formularies, negotiates rebates, runs pharmacy networks.

FormularyPayers

The list of drugs a plan covers and their tiers. Formulary position is what rebates buy.

Step therapyPayers

A requirement to try a cheaper drug before a more expensive one is covered.

Prior authorisationPayers

A requirement for approval from the plan before a drug is covered.

Spread pricingPayers

A PBM charging a plan more for a drug than it pays the pharmacy and keeping the difference.

DelinkingPayers

Paying PBMs flat service fees rather than amounts tied to drug prices or rebates.

Medicare Part DPayers

The Medicare outpatient prescription drug benefit, run by private plans.

Medicare Part BPayers

Medicare coverage including drugs administered in a doctor's office or hospital outpatient setting.

Maximum fair pricePayers

The price Medicare negotiates for a selected drug under the IRA.

Pill penaltyPayers

Industry term for the IRA's shorter negotiation clock for small molecules (9 years) than biologics (13).

TrumpRxPayers

A federal website, launched February 2026, listing discounted direct-to-consumer prices under MFN agreements.

rNPVEconomics

Risk-adjusted net present value: discounted cash flows weighted by the probability of reaching each stage.

Peak salesEconomics

A drug's highest annual revenue, typically five to seven years after launch.

Patent cliffEconomics

The sharp revenue fall when a major product loses exclusivity.

BiobucksEconomics

The headline value of a licensing deal including all potential milestones, most of which are never paid.

Milestone paymentEconomics

A payment due on a clinical, regulatory or sales event under a licensing deal.

Royalty monetisationEconomics

Selling a future royalty stream for cash today, as with Royalty Pharma.

Cash runwayEconomics

How long a biotech can operate on its cash before needing more funding.

Section 232 pharma tariffEconomics

A 100% tariff on imported patented drugs and APIs, with lower or zero rates for onshoring and MFN commitments.

Transfer pricingEconomics

The internal price at which a multinational sells product or licenses IP between its own entities.

Pillar TwoEconomics

The OECD 15% global minimum tax on large multinationals, in force in most major economies from 2024.

17 / What to retain

Twelve things worth keeping

If everything else evaporates, these survive.

1

Most drugs fail

About 8% of Phase 1 candidates reach approval. The cost of a drug is mostly the cost of its failed siblings.

2

Phase 2 is the valley

It is the first real test of the biology, and where most programmes die.

3

Value is risk retired

rNPV jumps at each successful readout. Biotech share prices are probability updates.

4

The revenue curve has a date

Exclusivity ends on a knowable day, and small-molecule sales fall by most of their value within a year.

5

Patents and exclusivities are different

One comes from the patent office, the other from the regulator. The later to expire sets the cliff.

6

List is not net

The gap exceeds $300 billion a year across US brands. Ask which price anyone is quoting.

7

Patients often pay on list

Coinsurance and deductibles computed on list price can make patients pay more than their plan's real cost share.

8

PBMs sell formulary access

Rebates buy position. Reforms now try to break the link between their pay and list prices.

9

Three wholesalers, one-percent margins

And better businesses than the margin suggests, mostly because of generics.

10

Cheap drugs have fragile supply

Shortages come from economics: no margin, no redundancy.

11

The US now sets prices too

IRA negotiation, MFN deals and tariff-linked pricing have ended the American exception.

12

IP lives where tax is low

Where patents are owned shapes where profit, and increasingly tariffs, are booked.

The question to ask first, always

When does this drug — or this company's largest drug — lose exclusivity, and what is its net price, not its list price? Most pharmaceutical disappointments, from collapsing revenue to a surprise policy hit, are visible in those two numbers long before they reach the share price.
